Neural Networks: An Executive Overview

Neural networks are function approximators built from many simple units. Given enough data and capacity, they learn to map inputs to outputs by adjusting connection weights to minimise a loss. Their strength is representation learning; their costs are data, compute, and interpretability.

How they work

Each neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias and applies a non-linear activation. Neurons are arranged in layers; hidden layers transform the input into progressively more useful features, and the output layer produces the prediction.

Training

Training uses gradient descent. Backpropagation computes how a small change in each weight affects the loss, and the optimiser nudges every weight in the direction that reduces error. This repeats over many batches of examples.

Where they fit

Neural networks excel at perception tasks — vision, speech, language — where hand-designed features are hard to specify. For small, tabular problems, simpler models are often competitive and easier to explain.

Implications

Adopting neural networks means investing in data pipelines, evaluation discipline, and monitoring. Model quality is only as good as the data and the objective it was trained on.

  • A network is many simple weighted units combined non-linearly.
  • Depth enables feature composition.
  • Training minimises a loss via gradient descent and backpropagation.
  • Data quality bounds model quality.
  • Interpretability and monitoring are ongoing costs.
